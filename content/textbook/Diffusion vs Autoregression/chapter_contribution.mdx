# Offline Behavioral Cloning for Robot Manipulation: Autoregressive Regression versus Denoising Diffusion

**Course:** CSCI 7000 Spring 2026
**Track:** Offline Policy Learning
**Primary Paper Audited:** Chi et al., "Diffusion Policy: Visuomotor Policy Learning via Action Diffusion," RSS 2023

---

## 1. The Audit: What Chi et al. Actually Claim and Where the Gaps Are

Chi et al. introduced Diffusion Policy as a visuomotor policy learning framework that models the action distribution using a conditional denoising diffusion probabilistic model (DDPM).
The core claim is that the multimodal expressiveness of DDPM outperforms unimodal Gaussian regression on contact-rich manipulation tasks.
This claim is empirically supported in their paper using simulation environments (PushT, Block Pushing, Robomimic) and a physical robot (Franka Panda).
What is not controlled for in their evaluations is the compute cost of $T = 100$ denoising steps at inference versus a single forward pass for direct regression.
They also do not test against a Transformer-based Gaussian regression baseline under identical data conditions.
The proprioception-only setting, which is the cheapest and most deployment-friendly configuration, is largely absent from their ablations.

Their implementation uses two backbone architectures: a CNN-based UNet and a Transformer-based diffusion network.
For the Transformer variant, they report $T = 100$ DDPM denoising steps at inference, generating a chunk of $T_a = 8$ future actions per call.
This action chunking strategy amortizes inference cost but introduces a receding-horizon execution loop.
We do not use action chunking in this work.
We test single-step prediction throughout, which is a deliberate simplification that isolates whether the denoising process itself adds value over regression when the task is not multimodal.

---

## 2. The Architectural Delta

### 2.1 Identified Bottleneck

The bottleneck in Chi et al. is the following: $T = 100$ denoising steps at inference is a multiplicative cost that scales linearly with model depth and batch size, and there is no empirical lower bound on $T$ reported in their paper for proprioceptive-only inputs.
Chi et al. justify $T = 100$ based on visual manipulation tasks where action distributions are plausibly multimodal (multiple grasp orientations, multiple approach paths).
For a bin-sorting task with a fixed target bin and deterministic expert demonstrations, the ground-truth action distribution is approximately unimodal conditioned on state.
The question becomes: does the denoising process provide any benefit on unimodal data, or does it just add variance and inference cost?

### 2.2 Technical Specification for the Change

**Change Proposed:** Replace $T = 100$ DDPM denoising with $T = 20$ and introduce a direct Gaussian regression baseline (AR Policy) under identical Transformer backbone conditions.

**Why this change is necessary:**

Inference time in robot control is constrained by the control loop frequency.
RoboTurkPilot demonstrations were collected at approximately 20 Hz.
At $T = 100$ denoising steps, with a 2-layer Transformer at $d = 256$, a single inference call on CPU takes on the order of 200ms per step, which exceeds the 50ms budget for 20 Hz control.
At $T = 20$, the same call takes approximately 40ms, which is within budget.
If $T = 20$ already saturates the quality ceiling imposed by unimodal data, then the additional 80 steps in the original paper are wasted compute on this task type.

**What this spec proves:**
A Gaussian regression model that outputs $\mu$ and $\sigma$ over the action in a single forward pass has zero denoising overhead.
If this baseline matches or beats $T = 20$ diffusion on a unimodal dataset, that is evidence the denoising process is contributing noise rather than expressiveness.
Our results confirm this: the AR policy achieves a test trajectory RMSE of 0.0282 against the diffusion policy's 0.1481 after 50 epochs of training, a 5x gap, under identical Transformer backbone conditions.

## 3. State Space and Action Space

### 3.1 Formal Definitions

Let $\mathcal{S} \subset \mathbb{R}^{73}$ be the proprioceptive state space.
Each state vector $\mathbf{s}_t \in \mathcal{S}$ includes joint positions, joint velocities, end-effector pose (position and orientation), the relative pose of the end-effector to the target object, and a scalar gripper state.
No visual observations are used.
This is a deliberate simplification relative to Chi et al., and we discuss its implications in Section 8.

The context window provides the model with a sequence of $W = 16$ recent states:

$$\mathbf{S}_t = [\mathbf{s}_{t-W+1}, \ldots, \mathbf{s}_t] \in \mathbb{R}^{W \times 73}$$

Steps at the beginning of a trajectory where $t < W$ are zero-padded, and a boolean padding mask $\mathbf{m}_t \in \{0, 1\}^W$ is passed to the Transformer to prevent attention over padded positions.

Let $\mathcal{A} \subset \mathbb{R}^8$ be the action space, parameterized as delta end-effector commands:

$$\mathbf{a}_t = [\Delta\mathbf{p}_t, \Delta\mathbf{q}_t, g_t] \in \mathbb{R}^3 \times \mathbb{R}^4 \times \mathbb{R}$$

where $\Delta\mathbf{p}_t \in \mathbb{R}^3$ is the position delta, $\Delta\mathbf{q}_t \in \mathbb{R}^4$ is the quaternion delta, and $g_t \in \mathbb{R}$ is the continuous gripper actuation (whose sign encodes open or closed).

Both $\mathbf{s}_t$ and $\mathbf{a}_t$ are z-score normalized per dimension using statistics computed from the training split only:

$$\tilde{\mathbf{s}}_t = \frac{\mathbf{s}_t - \boldsymbol{\mu}_s}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_s + \epsilon}, \quad \tilde{\mathbf{a}}_t = \frac{\mathbf{a}_t - \boldsymbol{\mu}_a}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_a + \epsilon}$$

where $\epsilon = 10^{-6}$ prevents division by zero on constant dimensions.

## 4. The Data Mix

### 4.1 Foundational Priors

We use no pre-trained visual encoders in this work.
There is no SigLIP, DINOv2 or ResNet-18 feature extractor in the pipeline.
This was done on purpose.
The goal is to isolate the contribution of the policy architecture from the contribution of the visual representation.
Including a pre-trained visual backbone would conflate two variables: the encoder expressiveness and the policy learning objective.
That conflation is present in Chi et al.'s full pipeline and makes it harder to attribute performance differences to the diffusion process itself.

If this work were extended to a visual setting, the encoder of choice would be DINOv2 ViT-B/14 over SigLIP, for one specific reason.
DINOv2 features trained on DINOv2 are well-suited to 3D spatial reasoning tasks because the self-supervised objective preserves local spatial structure.
SigLIP is language-image aligned, which is useful for instruction-conditioned policies but adds semantic noise for purely geometric manipulation tasks where no language grounding is needed.

### 4.2 Embodied Data

We use the bins-Bread subset of RoboTurkPilot, which is part of the Open X-Embodiment collection.
The full dataset contains 1069 demonstrations across a bin-sorting task where the robot moves a bread object into a target bin.
Trajectory lengths range from approximately 300 to 2000 steps, with a mean of 1065 steps.
This variance in trajectory length motivated the use of a stratified-length splitting strategy, described in Section 4.4.

We did not sample from DROID or any other dataset.
DROID contains language-annotated whole-arm trajectories from a Franka robot in diverse real-world settings.
The sim-to-real gap between RoboTurkPilot (MuJoCo simulation) and DROID (physical hardware with camera observations) makes mixing these datasets non-trivial without domain adaptation.
We leave cross-dataset training to future work.

### 4.3 Synthetic Multiplication

We did not use MimicGen or RoboGen.
The 1069 demonstrations provide approximately 1.17 million state-action pairs, which is sufficient for the model capacity we use (approximately 1.08M parameters for AR and 1.35M for diffusion).
Synthetic data would only matter at a scale that requires a much higher-capacity policy, a more complex task, or fewer than 100 demonstrations. 
None of those conditions apply here.

### 4.4 Curation Strategy

Demo selection uses stratified sampling by trajectory length.
Trajectory lengths are binned into 4 quantile buckets.
Demos are drawn uniformly across buckets until the requested count is reached.
Random sampling tends to over-represent short demonstrations, which skews the policy toward behaviors that appear early in trajectories. 
Stratified sampling fixes this.

The train-val-test split is 80-10-10, applied with the same stratification logic (856 train, 108 val, 105 test).
All normalization statistics are computed from the training split only, then applied to val and test at inference.

## 5. Loss Function Derivations

### 5.1 Autoregressive Policy Loss

The AR policy models the action distribution as a diagonal Gaussian conditioned on the state context:

$$\pi_\theta(\mathbf{a}_t \mid \mathbf{S}_t) = \mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_\theta(\mathbf{S}_t),\, \text{diag}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_\theta(\mathbf{S}_t)^2))$$

The Transformer encoder maps the padded context window $\mathbf{S}_t$ to a hidden representation $\mathbf{h}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{256}$ by extracting the final token after two self-attention layers:

$$\mathbf{h}_t = \text{LayerNorm}(\text{TransformerEncoder}(\mathbf{W}_\text{in}\mathbf{S}_t)_{[\text{last}]})$$

Two linear heads then produce the distribution parameters:

$$\boldsymbol{\mu}_\theta = \mathbf{W}_\mu \mathbf{h}_t + \mathbf{b}_\mu, \quad \log \boldsymbol{\sigma}_\theta = \text{clip}(\mathbf{W}_\sigma \mathbf{h}_t + \mathbf{b}_\sigma,\, -5,\, 2)$$

The model is trained to maximize the likelihood of the ground-truth action under the predicted distribution. The loss is summed across action dimensions and averaged over the batch:

$$\mathcal{L}_\text{AR} = -\mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{S}_t, \mathbf{a}_t) \sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{8} \log \mathcal{N}(a_{t,k} \mid \mu_{\theta,k}, \sigma_{\theta,k})\right]$$

Expanding the Gaussian log-likelihood:

$$\mathcal{L}_\text{AR} = \mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{8} \left(\frac{(a_{t,k} - \mu_{\theta,k})^2}{2\sigma_{\theta,k}^2} + \log \sigma_{\theta,k} + \frac{1}{2}\log 2\pi\right)\right]$$

The $\frac{1}{2}\log 2\pi$ term is a constant and does not affect the gradient.
The clamping on $\log \boldsymbol{\sigma}$ prevents two failure modes: variance collapse ($\log \sigma < -5$ produces near-zero variance and infinite loss spikes) and variance explosion ($\log \sigma > 2$ produces $\sigma > 7$, which effectively shuts off the regression signal by widening the distribution to the point where any action gets near-equal likelihood).

### 5.2 Diffusion Policy Loss

The diffusion policy learns to reverse a Markov forward process that progressively corrupts a clean action $\mathbf{a}_0$ with Gaussian noise.
The forward process is:

$$q(\mathbf{a}_t \mid \mathbf{a}_0) = \mathcal{N}(\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\mathbf{a}_0,\, (1 - \bar{\alpha}_t)\mathbf{I})$$

where $\bar{\alpha}_t = \prod_{i=1}^{t} \alpha_i$, $\alpha_i = 1 - \beta_i$, and $\beta_i$ follows a linear schedule from $\beta_1 = 10^{-4}$ to $\beta_T = 2 \times 10^{-2}$ over $T = 20$ steps.

In closed form, a noisy sample at step $t$ is:

$$\mathbf{a}_t = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\mathbf{a}_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}, \quad \boldsymbol{\epsilon} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})$$

The model $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta$ is trained to predict the noise $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$ given the noisy action, the diffusion timestep, and the state context.
The state context is encoded by the same Transformer backbone used in the AR policy:

$$\mathbf{c}_t = \text{StateEncoder}(\mathbf{S}_t) \in \mathbb{R}^{256}$$

The noisy action and timestep are projected and concatenated:

$$\mathbf{z} = [\mathbf{c}_t \;\|\; \mathbf{W}_a \mathbf{a}_t \;\|\; \mathbf{E}(t)] \in \mathbb{R}^{768}$$

where $\mathbf{W}_a \in \mathbb{R}^{256 \times 8}$ and $\mathbf{E}(t) \in \mathbb{R}^{256}$ is a learned timestep embedding.
A three-layer MLP with GELU activations maps $\mathbf{z}$ to the predicted noise:

$$\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{S}_t, \mathbf{a}_t, t) = \mathbf{W}_3\,\text{GELU}(\mathbf{W}_2\,\text{GELU}(\mathbf{W}_1 \mathbf{z}))$$

The training loss is the mean squared error between the predicted and actual noise, with $t$ sampled uniformly at each step:

$$\mathcal{L}_\text{Diff} = \mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{S}_t, \mathbf{a}_0) \sim \mathcal{D},\, t \sim \mathcal{U}[0, T-1],\, \boldsymbol{\epsilon} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})}\left[\left\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon} - \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{S}_t,\, \mathbf{a}_t,\, t)\right\|^2\right]$$

### 5.3 Combined Training Objective

In this work both models are trained independently on the same dataset using their respective losses.
There is no joint loss or shared optimization.
For completeness, the total loss across both policies (if trained jointly) would be:

$$\mathcal{L}_\text{total} = \lambda_\text{AR}\,\mathcal{L}_\text{AR} + \lambda_\text{Diff}\,\mathcal{L}_\text{Diff}$$

with $\lambda_\text{AR} = \lambda_\text{Diff} = 1$ in our setup (independent training, separate optimizers, same Adam configuration with $\text{lr} = 3 \times 10^{-4}$).

## 6. Intuitive Derivations

### 6.1 Why NLL for AR

The NLL loss does two things simultaneously that MSE cannot do alone.
First, it penalizes prediction error scaled by the model's own confidence: an incorrect prediction with high $\sigma$ is penalized less than an equally incorrect prediction with low $\sigma$.
Second, it forces the model to be honest about its uncertainty.
If the model sets $\sigma$ very large everywhere to avoid NLL penalties, the $\log \sigma$ term in the loss grows and punishes that strategy.
The result is that $\sigma$ converges to an estimate of the per-dimension prediction residual at the training set.

In contrast, MSE treats all dimensions and all timesteps equally.
For robot manipulation, the action dimensions have very different scales and variability.
The gripper dimension (dim 7) switches between approximately $-1$ and $+1$ at infrequent contact events.
The translational dimensions (dims 0-2) stay small throughout training, usually somewhere between 0.001 and 0.01. MSE on the raw normalized actions would technically work, but the Gaussian head gives better-calibrated outputs because it learns the variance separately for each dimension.

### 6.2 Why Noise Prediction over Score Matching for Diffusion

Ho et al. (DDPM, 2020) showed that predicting $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$ (the added noise) produces better samples than predicting $\mathbf{a}_0$ (the clean action) or the score $\nabla \log p(\mathbf{a}_t)$ directly.
The reason is that predicting $\mathbf{a}_0$ from heavily noised inputs at high $t$ is an ill-conditioned problem: the signal-to-noise ratio is low and the gradient signal to the network is weak.
Predicting $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$ keeps the regression target in a fixed $\mathcal{N}(0, I)$ distribution regardless of $t$, which stabilizes training.

For $T = 20$, the beta schedule from $10^{-4}$ to $2 \times 10^{-2}$ is compressed relative to Chi et al.'s $T = 100$ schedule.
At $t = 20$ (the final noise level), $\bar{\alpha}_{20} = \prod_{i=1}^{20}(1 - \beta_i) \approx 0.73$, meaning roughly 27% of the clean signal is destroyed.
This is much less noise than Chi et al.'s final noise level where $\bar{\alpha}_{100} \approx 0.01$.
The consequence is that our diffusion model operates in a low-noise regime even at maximum $t$, which reduces the expressiveness of the prior and may explain part of the performance gap.

## 7. Implementation Gotchas

**Padding mask semantics in PyTorch TransformerEncoder.**
PyTorch's `src_key_padding_mask` uses the convention that `True` means "ignore this position."
This is the opposite of the convention used in many papers where a mask value of 1 means "attend here."
In our `SequenceDataset`, the padding mask is initialized to all `True` and then set to `False` for the valid (non-padded) positions.
Flipping this convention silently produces incorrect attention over padded zeros, which inflates performance on early-trajectory steps where most positions are padded.

**Quaternion normalization drift.**
The action space includes a quaternion delta $\Delta\mathbf{q}_t \in \mathbb{R}^4$, but this delta is not constrained to unit norm during training or inference.
The model predicts an arbitrary $\mathbb{R}^4$ vector.
When accumulating deltas over a long trajectory, the quaternion representation drifts off the unit sphere.
In an online rollout, this would require periodic renormalization, which is not implemented in the current codebase because evaluation is offline (comparing predicted actions to ground-truth step-by-step, not accumulating state).

**Normalization statistics must come from training split only.**
`compute_normalization_stats` is called on `splits["train"]` before the val and test sets are used.
Calling it on the full dataset (a common mistake when iterating quickly) leaks information about the val and test distributions into the normalization, which artificially reduces the normalized residual.
The effect is small (roughly 0.001 RMSE on this dataset) but it violates the train/val/test assumption.

**Content-addressed NPZ cache.**
The embedding cache is content-addressed using SHA-256. `compute_embeddings` hashes the cache spec. 
HDF5 path, file size, mtime, max_demos, selection strategy, seed, and stratify_bins, and uses the first 12 hex characters of that hash in the cache filename. Change any of those fields and you get a new cache file; the old one stays. 
After a few runs with different `--max-demos` values, `data/processed/` will start accumulating multiple large NPZ files.
This is not a bug but it will silently fill disk.

**Diffusion val RMSE degrades after epoch 1 and never recovers.**
The best diffusion val RMSE is 0.1440 at epoch 1.
Over 50 epochs it rises monotonically to 0.1776, despite the noise prediction training loss continuing to decrease from 0.581 to 0.397 over the same period.
The AR training NLL tells the opposite story: it drops from 4.44 at epoch 1 to 0.17 at epoch 49, and the AR val RMSE tracks this improvement, reaching its best value of 0.0261 at epoch 50.
This is not overfitting in the classical sense for the diffusion model.
The noise prediction loss ($\mathcal{L}_\text{Diff}$) decreasing while val RMSE increases means the model is learning to predict noise more accurately in the training distribution, but this does not translate to better action reconstruction at $T = 20$ denoising steps.
The offline val RMSE is computed from the full denoising chain and the errors in each step compound.
As the model fits the noise distribution more tightly, the error compounding under the truncated schedule worsens rather than improves.

## 8. Lineage of Failure

### 8.1 Why $T = 20$ Was Not Enough

The choice of $T = 20$ was motivated by inference-time constraints (Section 2.2).
It turns out that $T = 20$ with a linear beta schedule is too compressed for the denoising chain to fully recover action structure.
At $T = 20$, the cumulative noise product $\bar{\alpha}_{20} \approx 0.73$ means the forward process never achieves near-isotropic Gaussian corruption.
The diffusion model is learning to denoise from a relatively mild noise level, which reduces the diversity of the sampling distribution and limits what the denoising process can express.

Chi et al. use $T = 100$ with a cosine noise schedule (following Nichol and Dhariwal, 2021), which reaches near-isotropic noise at $t = 100$.
We use a linear schedule, which has heavier tails at high $t$ and is known to produce suboptimal samples in image generation tasks.
Neither of these differences is controlled for.
The fair comparison would require matching $T$, schedule type, and backbone.

### 8.2 Why Proprioception Only Was a Limiting Choice

RoboTurkPilot demonstrations were collected with visual feedback from the operator.
The 73-dimensional state vector is derived from simulation ground-truth and contains information (exact object pose, relative EE-to-object distance) that would not be available in a physical deployment without a separate pose estimator.
This makes the benchmark easier than it appears.
A policy trained on this state representation cannot be directly transferred to hardware.

At the same time, using ground-truth state removes the visual encoder as a confound, which is what we wanted.
The tradeoff is that the results are not directly comparable to visuomotor policies.

### 8.3 What the Offline Metric Does Not Tell Us

The offline action prediction RMSE measures how well the model clones the expert's actions given ground-truth states.
It does not measure task completion.
A policy with RMSE 0.026 is not guaranteed to complete the bin-sorting task at a higher rate than a policy with RMSE 0.144.
Compounding errors in closed-loop rollout can turn a small per-step error into a complete trajectory failure.
Diffusion policies, by virtue of generating diverse samples, can in principle recover from perturbations that a point-estimate regression policy cannot.
This advantage is invisible in offline evaluation.

## 9. Results

### 9.1 Test Set Numbers

All results are on 105 held-out test demonstrations totaling 111,817 timesteps.
Best checkpoints are selected by minimum validation trajectory RMSE.
Results are shown for two training budgets: 5 epochs (early stopping) and 50 epochs (full run, `full_train_50_epochs`).
The diffusion best checkpoint is the epoch-1 checkpoint in both cases because val RMSE never improves past that point.
The AR best checkpoint is epoch 4 at 5 epochs and epoch 50 at 50 epochs.

| Metric | AR (5 ep) | AR (50 ep) | Diffusion (5 ep) | Diffusion (50 ep) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Trajectory RMSE (mean) | 0.0298 | 0.0282 | 0.1474 | 0.1481 |
| Trajectory RMSE (std) | 0.0126 | 0.0139 | 0.0208 | 0.0203 |
| Step RMSE | 0.0339 | 0.0334 | 0.1483 | 0.1487 |
| Step MAE | 0.0121 | 0.0094 | 0.0367 | 0.0368 |
| Mean step L2 | 0.0545 | 0.0439 | 0.2168 | 0.2176 |
| Cosine similarity | 0.9968 | 0.9970 | 0.9551 | 0.9547 |
| Gripper sign accuracy | 71.5% | 71.5% | 70.3% | 70.3% |
| Success at $\|\mathbf{e}\|_2 \leq 0.05$ | 67.4% | 77.0% | 35.4% | 35.1% |
| Success at $\|\mathbf{e}\|_2 \leq 0.10$ | 90.6% | 92.0% | 61.2% | 61.1% |
| Best val RMSE (epoch) | 0.0280 (ep 4) | 0.0261 (ep 50) | 0.1440 (ep 1) | 0.1440 (ep 1) |

**AR parameters:** 1,077,776 (all trainable).
**Diffusion parameters:** 1,345,800 trainable + 60 buffer floats ($\boldsymbol{\beta}$, $\boldsymbol{\alpha}$, $\bar{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}$, which are fixed schedule values).

### 9.2 What These Numbers Mean

The 5x RMSE gap persists regardless of training duration.
Extending training from 5 to 50 epochs improved the AR test trajectory RMSE from 0.0298 to 0.0282, a 5.4% improvement, and pushed success at $\|\mathbf{e}\|_2 \leq 0.05$ from 67.4% to 77.0%.
The diffusion policy moved by less than 0.001 RMSE across the same training budget.
Its best checkpoint is still epoch 1 in both the 5-epoch and 50-epoch runs.
The 50-epoch diffusion training loss of 0.397 is meaningfully lower than the 5-epoch value of 0.460, which confirms the model is learning something, but that learning does not improve action reconstruction quality under the $T = 20$ denoising chain.

The cosine similarity numbers (0.997 vs 0.955) tell you the two models are not qualitatively different in their understanding of which direction to move.
Both models get the direction mostly right.
The diffusion model fails on magnitude, not direction.
The results line up with the compounding error hypothesis. Each denoising step introduces a small error, and after 20 steps those errors add up across all 8 action dimensions. 

Gripper accuracy is basically the same between the two (71.5% vs 70.3%) and doesn't improve with more training.
The gripper is a low-frequency binary signal that switches at contact events.
Both models learn the gross switching pattern with similar accuracy.
Neither model is particularly good at gripper accuracy in absolute terms, which suggests the contact moments are genuinely hard to predict from proprioception alone.

## 10. What Would Actually Fix This

**Increase training set data:** Increase the number of demos. Currently the model is trained on 128 demos. This is small for Denoising diffusion probablistic models (DDPMs) to generalize from.

**For the diffusion model:** increase $T$ to at least 100, switch to a cosine noise schedule, and verify that $\bar{\alpha}_T$ is close to 0 (indicating full noise corruption at the end of the forward process).
The current $T = 20$ linear schedule leaves $\bar{\alpha}_{20} \approx 0.73$, which means the model never learns to generate from near-isotropic noise and the reverse process has limited range.

**For fair comparison:** use action chunking ($T_a = 8$ or 16 future actions per call) for the diffusion model, which is how Chi et al. actually deploy it.
Single-step diffusion prediction is not the intended use case in their paper.

**For deployment:** add online rollout evaluation in RoboSuite with task success rate as the primary metric.
Offline RMSE is a necessary but not sufficient condition for a usable policy.

**For visual inputs:** attach a frozen DINOv2 ViT-B/14 encoder to the state pipeline and add patch token cross-attention in the Transformer backbone.
This would replace the ground-truth object pose fields in the 73-dim state with learned visual features, making the pipeline deployable on hardware without a pose estimator.
